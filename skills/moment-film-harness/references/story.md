# Story

A launch film is one feature, one thing to remember, one viewer. Most bad launch films are good motion around no story:
a list of features, three cards in a row, a logo, a URL.

## 1. The one thing

- **What shipped**, in one line: "You can now …". A version or a whole-app release ("2.0", "what's new") is not one
  thing: open it and take its headline feature. A whole-company film only fits a company with one product.
- **The one thing** the viewer must remember after the film ends. It is usually the `after` in the viewer's words, not
  the brand slogan.
- **Who it is for and what they do**: one viewer doing one real task with the feature, from the moment they need it to
  the result. That task is the film's spine, and one real piece of data (a query, a file, a prompt) travels through it
  from the hook to the payoff.
- **Where it runs and what it asks**: the channel sets the ratio, the length and how fast the hook must land; the call
  to action is the last thing on screen.
- Keep product facts (what it does, real numbers, real labels) apart from creative choices (angle, tone, look). Every
  claim on screen traces back to the source: the site, the PR, the docs or the person's own words.
- Use the person's own words verbatim when they gave copy. Never "clean it up".

## 2. Storylines

Write three. They are three different films: each has its own **world** (where it happens, what it looks like) and its
own **device** (how the story is told). Three versions of the UI demo on a dark canvas with new titles count as one.
Make one of the three the bold one you would not have picked first.

Devices the official HyperFrames launch films use (`craft.md` §1 has how each is built):

| Device                         | What the viewer sees                                                                   | Seen in                          |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| A real moment in someone's day | a person, a time stamp, a message that creates the need ("launch is tomorrow")         | Studio                           |
| A character does the work      | a mascot or the agent's cursor carries, drags, draws and reacts; every cut has a cause | One Cent Per Second, Voice Debut |
| The feature as the hook        | the feature happening to a recognisable image in the first half second                 | Background Removal               |
| The result first               | a finished output plays, then the camera pulls back to show where it came from         | From Your Website                |
| One object across apps         | the thing being made travels from one app into the product, which keeps working on it  | Claude Motion                    |
| The product makes its own film | the film shows itself being built, then pulls back to reveal it inside the product     | Studio                           |
| Life before and after          | the old way as a scene (one person drowning in tabs), then the new way                 | the Desktop launch               |
| Type carries the argument      | giant kinetic type, colour fields and grids that cut on the beat                       | kinetic-type Moments on the wall |
| A number lands                 | one benchmark number earned by a scene, then made physical                             | Code2Video                       |

A device is what the story is. Its **form** is how the camera and the cuts carry it, and it changes a film as much:

| Form                       | What the viewer sees                                                                         |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| One continuous take        | the camera never cuts; it pushes, pulls and travels from one world into the next             |
| Object-to-object chain     | every shot ends on the thing that opens the next: a button becomes a card becomes a phone    |
| An endless zoom            | the camera dives into a detail that turns out to be the next world, again and again          |
| A flight through one world | a 3D set, a map or a desk the camera travels across; each feature is a stop on the route     |
| Cut on the beat            | hard cuts on the music, type and colour fields changing every half beat                      |
| Match cuts                 | one shape holds while everything around it changes: a circle becomes a lens, a wheel, a logo |
| Split screen to one        | several panes play at once, then collapse into the one that matters                          |
| One frame that rebuilds    | a single composition keeps rearranging itself and never leaves the frame                     |

Think wide before you choose. Sketch five or more ideas in a line each, pairing different devices with different
forms, and look on the wall for Moments that already do a form well (a camera that never cuts, a whip into the next
scene, a shape that morphs). Then drop the ideas this product cannot carry well, and of the rest keep the three that
differ most. A pitch where all three are plain cuts between scenes has not tried the forms.

- **Before and after**: the new side moves calmly and surely while the old side churns. Never mock a named competitor.
- **A recap** (a bundle of launches) gives the bundle one question first, so each piece answers it.
- Films in the same launch never share a device and a voice.

## 3. Structure: the beats

A common shape, for a film of about 35 s; scale it to yours, and let the storyline's device bend it:

| Beat     | Plan  | Job                                                                                             |
| -------- | ----- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Hook     | ~3 s  | the world and the need, or the feature already working; the feature's own term lands early      |
| Tension  | ~4 s  | the old way failing on screen, or a gag: the wait, the wall of text, the wrong result           |
| Reveal   | ~3 s  | the product or feature named as a title ("Introducing …", or its name), about 20–30% in         |
| Proof    | ~15 s | the real task in the real UI, as 3–6 verbs (_paste, pick, watch it build, ship_), one beat each |
| Payoff   | ~7 s  | the output arrives, already moving; the biggest visual moment of the film                       |
| Claim    | ~3 s  | the `after`, the one thing, in one line                                                         |
| End card | ~3 s  | the brand mark carried in, the product name and the call to action land and hold                |

In each verb beat keep only what the cursor touches and what proves the line; delete the rest of the screen. Subtraction
is the design act.

A result-first film plays the payoff as its hook and pulls back to the product; a late reveal names the product only at
the end card. Either way the mark lands once (§7).

## 4. Time

- The hook lands in the first second or two, and the first seconds are the densest of the film. A scene never sits
  unchanged for long: when a title holds, the world around it acts (footage plays, the UI works, a character reacts).
- The process runs at double speed; the payoff plays at normal speed.
- The biggest visual moment is the payoff. It happens once, about 60–80% of the way in.
- Length follows the story, usually 20–45 s for a feature launch and up to 80 s for a whole product. A length in the
  request is a range, not a cap. Never pad.
- A line stays long enough to read, about 0.3 s per word plus 0.5 s, then leaves with the scene.

## 5. Copy

- At most 6 words a line, one line on screen at a time, no trailing periods.
- Show, don't list: no three-card sequences, no bullet slides. Large centred type, or the product itself. When similar
  things repeat (cards, results, postcards), each one gets its own idea and layout, never one template with new words.
- Commands and URLs as short as they can be. A feature with a one-line command: type it in the UI on screen (and say it,
  if narrated): the command is the visible cause.
- Real numbers only, from the source. No invented metrics, customers or quotes.
- The first line is never "Introducing", "Meet", "Today we're launching" or the logo. After the pain, naming the product
  with "Introducing X" is fine.
- Banned: "unlock the power of", "seamless", "supercharge", "game-changer", "revolutionize", "next-level", "effortless",
  "in seconds" (unless measured).
- Read the lines in order with no pictures: a stranger must get the pitch. Then read the pictures with no lines: they
  must tell the same story.

## 6. Voice or silent

- **Music only** is the default for a launch film; the official films are almost all music only. Without a voice, the
  type and the UI's own text carry the words: large titles, few of them.
- **Narrated** when the person asks, or the film explains how something works; the voice-over is the most important part
  of an explainer. Draft it with the angle (approved on the storyboard when it is on), and it becomes the clock
  (`sound.md`). The screen never repeats the spoken sentence word for word: the line is the short version.
- Captions only with a voice.

## 7. The brand

- The brand mark lands once, with weight, in the `brandReveal` beat, tagged `data-brand-mark`: usually the end card,
  sometimes a mid-film title. Before it, the brand shows only inside the product and the story (the app icon in a dock,
  the name in a generated title). It never docks small in a corner for the rest of the film.
- The end card is built, not a still: the mark is carried in (a character lifts it, an object becomes it, it is typed
  or wiped in), the product name and the call to action land, and it holds about 2–3 s. Build it once as a shared file;
  later films reskin it. The brand's own logo outro, when there is one, is used as it is.
- The accent colour is rationed: one highlighted word, the payoff, the end card.
